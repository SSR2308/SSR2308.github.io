Sea the Impact

Project Lead — Eagle Scout, Boy Scouts of America Installed at the Bolsa Chica Conservancy
7 ft
Whale sculpture, built from beach debris
90 lbs
Usable debris collected via community cleanups
~7 months
From first pitch to installation

A different kind of project from the rest of this site — no code, no CAD software, just a scout project that turned into a fabrication, design, and community-organizing problem all at once. The short version: a seven-foot whale sculpture built entirely from beach trash, now on permanent public display.

Overview

For my Eagle Scout project, I wanted to do more than the standard bench-building or landscaping project. At a beach cleanup earlier that year, our group spent two hours untangling washed-up seaweed from plastic bags, fishing line, bottle caps, styrofoam, and other debris — over 80 pounds of it in a single morning. That was the starting point: instead of just cleaning a beach once, build something that puts ocean pollution in front of people permanently. Inspired by the Washed Ashore marine art project, I decided to build a whale sculpture entirely out of collected beach debris and get it installed somewhere the public would actually see it.

Finding a home for it

Before any building could start, the project needed a beneficiary willing to display it — and that turned out to be its own months-long problem. Pitches went out to museums, environmental organizations, and city councils, and the rejections (or silence) piled up; a teenager's proposal to install trash art in a public space was, understandably, a hard sell without something concrete to show. I put together pitch decks, presentations, and social posts to make the case, and kept going after each rejection. The Bolsa Chica Conservancy eventually agreed to host it — which is where the real work, and the real deadline, began.

Collecting the material

I organized multiple community beach cleanups to gather the raw material, coordinating volunteers around conflicting schedules and turning what started as informal weekend cleanups into a repeatable process. In total, the cleanups collected 90 pounds of usable debris — plastic, metal, and other washed-ashore material sorted for what could actually be built with, not just hauled to a landfill.

Designing and building the sculpture

Design took more iteration than I expected. Early versions were too large to be practical, later ones too heavy to actually mount and display; getting the tail shape and the debris placement to read clearly as a whale, rather than just a pile of trash, took several restarts before a design balanced aesthetics against what was structurally and logistically buildable.

Once the design was set, I still had no way to actually build at that scale — my robotics background helped with the mechanical thinking, but not with the tools or shop space a seven-foot metal-framed sculpture needed. After a round of cold emails, a local workshop agreed to lend their space and guidance. That summer was spent bending metal and building the frame, then cleaning and prepping every individual piece of debris — scrubbing off patina and grime so each piece could be identified and placed deliberately rather than just embedded as anonymous trash. One dented, mud-caked aluminum can — nearly thrown out before a scoutmaster pointed out it still had a place in the piece — ended up cleaned up and placed as part of the sculpture's centerpiece.

Outcome

The finished whale sculpture now stands permanently at the Bolsa Chica Conservancy, where visitors see it as both public art and a close-up look at what actually washes up on Southern California beaches. Beyond the sculpture itself, running the project end to end — securing a beneficiary with no track record to point to, coordinating volunteers, adapting the design around real material and budget constraints, and getting a fabrication done in a borrowed shop — was as much the project as the welding was.
