A different kind of project from the rest of this site, less code, more welding, pitching, and community organizing. The short version: a seven-foot whale sculpture, designed in CAD after first proving the concept with a toothpick-and-bubble-wrap prototype, then hand-built out of real beach trash and installed permanently at the Bolsa Chica Conservancy.

Overview

For my Eagle Scout project, I wanted to do more than the standard bench-building or landscaping project. Inspired by the Washed Ashore marine-art installations, I set out to build a whale sculpture entirely out of collected beach debris and get it installed somewhere the public would actually see it, not just clean a beach once, but put ocean pollution in front of people permanently. That meant running the whole thing as a real project: securing a beneficiary, fundraising, organizing cleanups, designing the piece in CAD, and then fabricating it at a metal shop over a summer of weekend sessions.

The finished piece, every bottle, cap, and can inside the ribcage is real debris collected from the cleanups

Finding a beneficiary

Before any building could start, the project needed an organization willing to display it, and that turned out to be its own months-long problem. I put together a full project-proposal board (phases, budget, fundraising plan, beneficiary research) and pitched it at an initial benefactor meeting; a teenager's proposal to install trash art in a public space was, understandably, a hard sell without something concrete to show. The Bolsa Chica Conservancy eventually agreed to host it at their Ecological Reserve Interpretive Center, which is where the real deadline began.

The pitch, a five-phase plan covering cleanup, design, build, and installation, with a full budget breakdown

Fundraising & collecting the material

Materials, the acrylic display case, and shop costs were funded through a Troop 604 root beer float sale, a church donut Sunday, and a GoFundMe page. The raw material came from organized beach cleanups at the Bolsa Chica Ecological Reserve and Corona del Mar / Newport Beach, volunteers in high-vis vests working the dunes and shoreline, weighing what was collected, then sorting it into what could actually be built with versus what just needed to be disposed of properly. Cleaned pieces, cans, bottles, caps, were scrubbed, rinsed, and hung to dry before being cataloged for the build.

Collect, weigh, sort, clean, dry — every piece that made it into the sculpture went through this before the shop ever saw it

Designing the sculpture (CAD)

Design started low-tech, on purpose: before touching CAD, I built a small physical prototype out of toothpicks and a scrap of bubble wrap to work out the basic rib-and-spine structure a free-standing whale skeleton would need. Once that geometry made sense, I modeled the full sculpture myself in Onshape, a 6-foot-long, 2-foot-wide ribbed hull with a rotating tail and pectoral fins, mounted 2'4" up on a base, which became the reference drawing the shop and I worked from for every measurement afterward.

The first "model" — toothpicks and bubble wrap, before any CAD was opened

Side / top / front views from the CAD model — the dimensioned drawing the shop built to

Building at Handcrafted Metalworks

I had the design; I didn't have a metal shop. After a round of cold emails, Handcrafted Metalworks in Santa Ana agreed to let me build there under their guidance. The CAD model got reviewed on-screen with their team first, then traced full-scale onto foam board on the shop table with the actual rib spacing and measurements marked out, the same drawing the rest of the build followed. From there it was cutting stock to length, bending each rib to the drawing's arcs, and welding them one at a time into the ribcage, tail, and pectoral fins, then fitting a painted wood base to mount it on.

The full-scale template — rib positions and measurements marked directly from the CAD drawing

Ribs bent and welded one at a time, then fins and tail attached, then mounted on the finished base - still empty at this point

Last step — packing in the cleaned, sorted debris from the cleanups, piece by piece

Outcome

The finished whale sculpture now stands permanently at the Bolsa Chica Conservancy's Interpretive Center, where visitors see it as both public art and a close-up look at what actually washes up on Southern California beaches, every bottle cap and can inside it is real collected debris, not a prop. The plaque beside it reads: "Every piece of trash tells a story. Let's change the ending. Before you toss it… think again. Let this whale remind us small choices create big waves."

Beyond the welding, running the project end to end, securing a beneficiary with no track record to point to, fundraising, organizing volunteers across multiple cleanups, designing in CAD, building in a borrowed shop, and getting it installed and accepted as a permanent exhibit, was as much the project as the fabrication itself.
